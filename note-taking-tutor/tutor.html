Start here

Notes are a thinking tool, then a testing tool.

Most students treat notes as a copy of what the teacher said. Learning research points to something better: notes that make you think while you write them, and that let you quiz yourself afterward. This notebook trains both, one mission at a time, with feedback on your actual notes.

Your end goal

Take a page of handwritten notes from any class that captures the big ideas in your own words, shows how they connect, and turns into a self-quiz you can review in 10 minutes.

How each mission works

  • Goal: one skill, stated up front
  • Why: the research, briefly
  • See it: weak vs. strong notes, side by side
  • Try it: write on real paper, then enter it here
  • Feedback: instant checks, an AI coach, and a rubric
  • Lock it in: a quick quiz from memory

Keep a pen and paper next to you. Every practice task asks you to write by hand first. Then you type exactly what you wrote (arrows, abbreviations and all) or, when the AI coach is on, take a photo of the page. The feedback is only useful if it is about what you really wrote.

Warm-up: what do you already believe? guess first, then check

Make a call on each statement before you read the explanation. Guessing before you learn something helps you remember the answer, even when your guess is wrong (Richland, Kornell & Kao, 2009).

Mission 1 of 6

Think, don't transcribe

Goal

Turn a dense, textbook-style sentence into a short note in your own words that you'd still understand a month from now.

You've got it when

  • Your note is under about 40% of the original's length
  • No stretch of 5+ words is copied, except a definition you choose to quote
  • It shows a relationship (→, because, ∴) and keeps an example

Why this works

When you copy word for word, your hand does the work and your brain doesn't have to. When you put an idea in your own words, you have to understand it first, and understanding is what gets stored in memory. Psychologists call this the generation effect: information you produce yourself is remembered better than information you just read (Slamecka & Graf, 1978). Paraphrased and summarized notes lead to better learning than verbatim notes (Bretzing & Kulhavy, 1979).

A famous study found that students who typed lecture notes on laptops copied more verbatim and did worse on conceptual questions than students who wrote by hand (Mueller & Oppenheimer, 2014). Larger follow-up studies found the handwriting advantage is small or uncertain (Morehead, Dunlosky & Rawson, 2019; Urry et al., 2021). What holds up is the underlying point: notes help when they make you process ideas. Handwriting is slower, so it pushes you to select and rephrase. This mission trains that habit on purpose.

Sources are listed in The research.

Biology textbook

“Mitochondria generate most of the cell's supply of ATP through cellular respiration, a process that requires oxygen and breaks down glucose; cells with high energy demands, such as muscle cells, contain far more mitochondria than less active cells.”

Compression moves: cut filler words (“a process that”), swap phrases for symbols (→, =, ↑), keep one concrete example, and turn a fact into a why question.

Mission 2 of 6

Textbook notes: read, close the book, recall

Goal

Use the Preview → Read → Recall → Check routine to make Cornell notes from a textbook section, writing most of them with the book closed.

You've got it when

  • You wrote 2 or more preview questions before reading
  • Your from-memory notes caught most key ideas
  • You added corrections in a different color after checking
  • Your page has cue questions and a 2 to 3 sentence summary

Why this works

Highlighting and rereading feel productive, but a major review of learning techniques rated both as low utility (Dunlosky et al., 2013). The text looks familiar, so you think you know it. Recognizing something isn't the same as being able to recall it.

In one experiment, students who read a passage, closed the book and recited what they remembered, then reread it, beat students who took notes with the book open and students who just reread it twice (McDaniel, Howard & Einstein, 2009). Writing from memory is retrieval practice, and it shows you exactly what you don't know yet.

Skimming headings first and turning them into questions helps too. Trying to answer a question before you've learned the answer makes the answer stick better when you find it (Richland, Kornell & Kao, 2009).

See it a Cornell page

The Cornell format (Pauk) splits the page into three parts: notes on the right, cue questions in a narrow left column, and a summary at the bottom. You write the notes while reading. You add the cues and summary afterward, and they turn the page into a self-test. Here's one for a section on photosynthesis:

Cues (add after)

What goes in? What comes out?

Where does it happen?

Why must the light rxns come first?

Notes (from memory, then checked)

Photosynth. = light energy → chem. energy (glucose)

IN: CO₂ + H₂O + light → OUT: glucose + O₂

where: chloroplasts (chlorophyll absorbs light)

2 stages:

1. light rxns → make ATP + NADPH

2. Calvin cycle → uses them to build glucose from CO₂

+ light rxns in thylakoids, Calvin in stroma

∴ Calvin depends on light rxns

Summary (your own words)

Plants use light in chloroplasts to turn CO₂ + water into sugar + O₂ in 2 linked steps: light rxns make energy carriers that the Calvin cycle spends to build glucose.

The red line was added after checking the book: something missed during recall, fixed in a different color so you can see what to study.

Mission 3 of 6

Lecture notes, live

Goal

Keep up with a lecture you can't pause or rewind. Catch the main ideas by listening for the teacher's signals and writing in shorthand.

You've got it when

  • You caught the definition, both categories, all 3 factors, and the revenue idea
  • Your notes show the lecture's structure (numbered factors, indented examples)
  • You flagged gaps with “?” instead of stopping

Why this works

Lecture note-taking is one of the hardest things students do. You listen, decide what matters, rephrase it and write, all while the teacher keeps going. Researchers measured it as more mentally demanding than ordinary reading and in the same range as expert chess play (Piolat, Olive & Kellogg, 2005). Because of that load, students often write down fewer than half of a lecture's important ideas (Kiewra, 1985), and you can't be tested well on ideas you never recorded.

Two things lower the load. First, teachers signal what matters (“there are three factors…”, “the key point is…”). Students who listen for these organizational cues take better notes and learn more (Titsworth & Kiewra, 2004). Second, shorthand and a keep-moving rule: when you miss something, leave a gap, mark it “?”, and fill it in after class. Revising notes afterward improves both the notes and test scores (Luo, Kiewra & Samuelson, 2016).

Mission 4 of 6

Show the structure

Goal

Pick the note format that matches how the ideas are related, and build a comparison matrix from a textbook passage.

You've got it when

  • You can match content to outline, matrix, flowchart, concept map or sketch
  • Your matrix fills all 9 cells with short phrases
  • You can read a pattern down a column

Why this works

Plain lists hide relationships. When ideas are compared across the same features, a matrix (a grid with topics as rows and features as columns) lets you see every comparison at once. Students who studied from matrix-style organizers learned relationships better than those who studied from outlines of the same material (Kiewra et al., 1991; Robinson & Kiewra, 1995).

Pictures help too. Memory stores words and images in partly separate ways, so a quick sketch adds a second route back to the idea (Paivio's dual coding theory). In experiments, drawing a concept beat writing it out for later recall, even for people who couldn't draw well (Wammes, Meade & Fernandes, 2016).

Earth Science · 3.2
The Rock Cycle's Three Families

Geologists sort rocks into three families by how they form. Igneous rocks form when molten rock cools and hardens. If magma cools slowly underground, large crystals have time to grow, producing coarse-grained rocks such as granite; lava that cools quickly at the surface produces fine-grained or glassy rocks such as basalt and obsidian.

Sedimentary rocks form when fragments of other rocks, shells, or minerals are deposited in layers, then compacted and cemented together over time. They often show visible layers and are the only rocks that commonly contain fossils; sandstone, shale, and limestone are examples.

Metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks are transformed by heat and pressure deep underground without melting. The pressure often lines minerals up into bands or flat sheets, a texture called foliation; marble (from limestone) and slate (from shale) are examples.

Mission 5 of 6

Revise, quiz, space

Goal

Turn your Mission 3 lecture notes into a self-test: revise them, write cue questions that make you explain, quiz yourself, and schedule spaced reviews.

You've got it when

  • You have 4 or more cue questions, at least half starting with why, how, what if or compare
  • You've done one round of cover-and-recall
  • You have a review plan with dates

Why this works

Notes do two jobs. Writing them helps you learn (the encoding job), and reviewing them later helps even more (the storage job). Reviewing notes matters at least as much as taking them (Kiewra, 1985). How you review matters most. Testing yourself beats rereading by a wide margin. In one study, students who practiced recalling a passage remembered far more a week later than students who reread it, even though the rereaders felt more confident (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). Retrieval practice even beat building concept maps (Karpicke & Blunt, 2011).

Spacing is the other half. The same study time spread across several days beats cramming for long-term memory (Cepeda et al., 2006). A good rule of thumb is to space reviews about 10 to 20% of the time until you need the material, so for a test in 10 days, review every day or two (Cepeda et al., 2008).

Good cue questions ask why and how, not just what. Explaining why a fact is true improves memory for it (Pressley et al., 1987; Dunlosky et al., 2013).

See it weak vs. strong cue questions

The 24-hour revision checklist. Within a day of class, and with a different color pen: (1) fill every “?” gap from the textbook or a classmate, (2) spell out any abbreviation you might forget, (3) add cue questions in the left margin, (4) write a 2 to 3 sentence summary at the bottom.
